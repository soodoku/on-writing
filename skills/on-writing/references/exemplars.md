# Exemplars: writing worth reverse-engineering

Pinker's first rule: reverse-engineer what you read. If it feels like good writing, work out what makes it good. These short passages are calibration targets. Before editing, skim the group that matches the register; after editing, ask whether the result moved toward them or away.

One light conditionality, and only one: **argument spends the reader's time, narrative earns it.** Argument prose opens with the point and stays frugal. Narrative prose may build, withhold, and repeat for effect; its details are the argument. The clarity rules (small words, concrete nouns, active verbs) hold in both. Don't over-apply the distinction: most good writing in either register is plain, specific, and forward-moving.

## Argument

**Paul Sniderman and Thomas Piazza, *The Scar of Race* (1993), opening line:**

> It is time to look again at how Americans think about the issue of race.

*The move:* one plain sentence carrying the whole agenda: subject, claim, stakes. Thirteen words, none over two syllables. No literature review, no "scholars have long debated." The book starts where a weaker book would arrive on page ten.

**The same book's thesis, in the register the authors use on themselves** (as summarized in review; the phrasing is the reviewer's, the register is theirs):

> Lots of ordinary folks know it, but it's not the sort of thing social scientists tend to say: when it comes to questions of public policy, few whites are racists.

*The move:* colloquial diction aimed at a technical audience, and a hard claim stated without insulation. The plainness is what makes the claim checkable. Its failure mode is real and reviewers named it: stark contrast can flatten a genuinely conditional finding. Take the flatness, keep the conditionality that is true.

**Robert Luskin, title of his 1991 reply on standardized coefficients:**

> Abusus Non Tollit Usum

*The move:* the title is the argument. Abuse does not negate proper use - three words that state the paper's entire position before the abstract begins.

**Robert Luskin, *Robert's Rules: Suggestions for Writing*:**

> Tell us what you think! The best papers have no literature review section, the best books no literature review chapter. This is not to say that the literature should not inform your thinking, nor that it should go uncited. But it should be in the background. What should occupy the foreground is the story you are telling—an amalgam uniquely your own.

> Be concise. Avoid unnecessary words. ... Spend words as though they were money, and you were a miser. If in doubt, leave it out.

Advice that demonstrates itself: short declaratives, one thought per sentence, a metaphor that earns its keep. Note the em dash used well; a human writer's dash is not a tell.

**John Cochrane, *Writing Tips for Ph.D. Students*:**

> A good paper is not a travelogue of your search process. We don't care how you came to figure out the right answer. We don't care about the hundreds of things you tried that did not work. Save it for your memoirs.

Deliberate repetition ("We don't care") as structure, then a four-word punchline in the stress position. Rhythm doing the work that signposting would do badly.

## Narrative

**V.S. Naipaul, *A Bend in the River* (1979), opening line:**

> The world is what it is; men who are nothing, who allow themselves to become nothing, have no place in it.

Nothing over two syllables until "allow." A whole philosophy in two clauses, and the weight lands on the final phrase. Small words compel precision; this is Naipaul's own rule for beginners, obeyed by its author.

**David Remnick, *Lenin's Tomb* (1993):**

> In the lobby, an old woman with legs wrapped in elastic bandages mopped the floor with filthy water. She kept missing the same spot, over and over. There was the overpowering smell of disinfectant, bad tobacco, and wet wool. This was the smell of Russia indoors, the smell of the woman in front of you on line, the smell of every elevator. Near an abandoned newsstand, dozens of overcoats hung on long rows of pegs, somber and dark, lightly steaming, like nags in a stable.

The observation is the argument: no thesis sentence about Soviet decay, just bandaged legs, wet wool, and steaming overcoats. Hard-to-fake specifics (what the skill's Mode B calls *cost*), and a closing simile that would be purple anywhere else but is earned by the five concrete sentences before it. An editor who "tightened" this would be vandalizing it.

## What the exemplars have in common

Across registers: the first sentence commits; details are specific enough to be checkable or smellable; sentences vary in length but each makes one statement; the fanciest word is the right word, not the biggest; and nothing announces what it is about to do. Edit toward that.
