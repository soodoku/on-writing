# Argument craft: the sources behind Passes 1 and 2

Load this for papers, memos, reports, and any writing whose job is to convince.
Passes 1 and 2 carry the rules that always apply; this is the rest, with the
reasoning that makes each rule stick and the source it comes from.

Four documents, all in [soodoku/on-writing](https://github.com/soodoku/on-writing):
Luskin's [*Robert's Rules*](https://github.com/soodoku/on-writing/blob/master/lit/Roberts-Rules-January-2013.pdf),
Cochrane's [*Writing Tips for Ph.D. Students*](https://github.com/soodoku/on-writing/blob/master/lit/phd_paper_writing.pdf),
the [*QJPS* style guidelines](https://github.com/soodoku/on-writing/blob/master/lit/QJPS-Style-Guidelines.pdf),
and Shafer's [*The Academic Whodunit*](https://github.com/soodoku/on-writing/blob/master/lit/shafer_1.MP.jpg).
They agree more than they differ, which is itself evidence.

---

## 1. Shafer: the paper as a whodunit with the answer given away

Shafer's frame is the most useful thing anyone has said about organizing an
argument, because it converts "be well organized" into a test you can apply.

A whodunit opens with a body. Within a sentence or two the reader thinks *well,
hello, what have we here* - and then asks, in order, what happened, why, and
with what consequences. The rest of the book answers those questions in exactly
the order the opening raised them.

An argument works the same way with one difference: **you give away the
murderer immediately.** From the first paragraph the reader knows what the
question is, why it matters, what the disagreement is, how you will settle it,
and what you conclude.

The payoff is a cutting rule. The opening defines what may follow, so anything
not needed to unmask the murderer gets killed, however much you like it. What
impresses is not a catalogue of everything you read but a no-frills
presentation of exactly what your case requires. This is why the opening
paragraph is the hardest thing to write and deserves the most passes: it is
doing double duty as argument and as filter.

## 2. Luskin: writing it well

The rules Passes 1 and 2 already carry are marked ✓. The rest are here.

**Put yourself in the foreground.**

- ✓ *Tell us what you think.* The best papers have no literature review section
  and the best books none. The literature informs your thinking and gets cited;
  it does not occupy the foreground. The theory should be yours.
- *Immerse, then step back.* Decide what you want to say, inspired by the
  literature but identical to nothing in it. Every citation should serve your
  argument or disarm an objection; anything else goes.
- ✓ *Cite; do not quote.* Use the thoughts, not the words. The exceptions are
  rare: when the exact words are wrong and you are catching the author, or when
  they are too good to paraphrase. Stringing quotes together, however apposite,
  discourages original thought.
- ✓ *Never write a sentence about what someone else thinks.* No "According to
  X", "As X says", "X shows that". Say what you think; cite X in parentheses.

**Organize so the transitions disappear.**

- ✓ *One point at a time.* Necessary qualifications come later, in their turn.
- ✓ *Paragraphs are units of thought*, a quarter to a half page. One thought per
  paragraph, one paragraph per thought. Mingling two thoughts, or spreading one
  across three paragraphs, hides the line of argument.
- *Use headings.* They show the structure and remove the need for transitions.
- ✓ *The best prose needs few explicit transitions*, because the sentences are
  sequenced so linearly that the logic is obvious. A long passage explaining the
  organization is a sign the organization is bad. The most elegant transitions
  are implicit.

**Be clear, then be brief.**

- ✓ *Spend words as though they were money and you were a miser.* If in doubt,
  leave it out.
- ✓ *Trust the reader.* Assume someone intelligent, informed as an intelligent
  lay reader would be, and paying attention.
- ✓ *Avoid saying what goes without saying.* Nobody reading a paper on Senate
  elections needs to be told what the Senate is. Unnecessary thoughts, however
  economically expressed, are no better than unnecessary words.
- ✓ *Never say anything twice.* Hardly ever. If it was clear the first time, it
  was remembered.
- *Try for colour, but not much.* Similes and metaphors are welcome and the
  unusual telling word is welcome. Most phrases still have to be literal and
  most words ordinary.
- ✓ *Keep sentences forward-moving*; hive off qualifications. Then combine the
  short ones for economy.
- ✓ *Avoid the passive*, except to de-emphasize an actor for reasons of genuine
  unimportance or diplomacy.

**Sentence mechanics worth knowing.**

- ✓ *Most emphatic position is the end; second is the beginning.* "Jones made
  mistakes but won" and "Jones won but made mistakes" report the same facts and
  mean different things. Holds for phrases, sentences, paragraphs, sections.
- ✓ *Parallel constructions for similarities and differences, and elide the
  repeats.* "He always had a secret yearning for a contemplative life, she for a
  life of toil."
- *Compress relative clauses.* Drop "that" where clarity survives. Replace
  pronoun-plus-verb with a participle: "the party that generally benefits from
  unionization" becomes "the party generally benefiting from unionization".
  Turn nonrestrictive clauses into appositives: "Jesse Ventura, who had
  previously been a professional wrestler" becomes "Jesse Ventura, previously a
  professional wrestler".
- *Group shared subjects and verbs.* Three sentences of the form "A does x to B.
  A does y to C. A does z to D" become "A does x to B, y to C, and z to D".
  Writers who don't do this usually compound the error by varying the words for
  A each time, which muddies the meaning further.
- *Drop context-setting phrases once context is set.* In a paper about the 2006
  election you never need "in the 2006 election" again.
- ✓ *Same words for the same thought.* Elegant variation invents distinctions
  that aren't there.
- ✓ *"Not all x are y"*, never "all x are not y", which literally says no x is y.
  Ninety-nine times in a hundred the writer means the former.
- ✓ *Begin sentences with "but"* for strong opposition. Never with "however" as
  a conjunction; move it inside. Same for "also" meaning "in addition", and
  "therefore" (try "hence" or "thus", or move it).
- *Terminal prepositions are fine.* "That depends on what they are cut with" is
  not improved by contortion.
- *Confine citations to parentheses or footnotes*, unless you are discussing
  that work at length.

**Diction to strike:** utilize (use), in terms of (in), in order to (to), the
fact that (that), upon (on), all of the (all the), there is / there are
(reword), oftentimes (often), throughout (usually sheer nimiety).

**Usage most often got wrong:** *data* and *media* are plural; *i.e.* restates,
*e.g.* exemplifies; *may* is present and unconditional, *might* past or
conditional; *as* introduces clauses, *like* introduces nouns; *who* for people,
*that* for things; put *only* next to what it modifies; don't split infinitives;
*stand* for a position on an issue, *stance* for a general posture; prefer the
possessive ("the other team's strategy"); prefer pronouns to repeated names.

**Getting it written** (Luskin's first section, for when you are drafting rather
than editing): write fast in multiple drafts and don't try too hard on any one;
on the first draft just slap something down, patchy is fine, you need something
to work with; edit in many passes, being content with improvement rather than
perfection on each; if stuck, move to another section or another project and
come back; don't get attached to anything you have written; keep your focus
wide, because a bad sentence is often a bad paragraph and a bad paragraph often
a badly organized section; when the writing is flowing, keep writing even at the
cost of other things; write something every day, because conditioning goes after
two or three days off.

## 3. Cochrane: the empirical paper

Overlaps Luskin on organization and adds a whole dimension on exhibits.

- *One central contribution, written down in one paragraph before you draft.*
  Distilling it is painful because you discover how much has to go.
- *Readers skim and no one reads start to finish.* Most want the basic result;
  a few want to know how it differs from others; almost nobody cares whether it
  holds under a different instrument set.
- ✓ *Triangular, not joke-structured.* A joke has a windup and a punchline. Put
  the punchline first and then explain the joke.
- ✓ *A paper is not a travelogue of your search process.* Nobody cares about the
  hundreds of things that did not work. Save it for your memoirs.
- *Abstract: say what you find, not what you look for.* No literature in it.
- *The introduction starts with what you do,* and explains it rather than only
  stating the conclusion. Do not open with philosophy, with how long the field
  has been interested in the topic, or with a long policy motivation. All of it
  is throat-clearing.
- *Order, at every scale: describe what you do, explain it, compare it to
  alternatives, compare it to others' procedures.* Most writers do exactly the
  reverse.
- ✓ *Previews and recalls are symptoms of misordering,* not aids to the reader.
- *Footnotes:* if it matters, put it in the text; if it does not, delete it. A
  parenthetical footnote means you have not found where the thought belongs.
- *Strike "I leave x for future research."* And never call work "illustrative" -
  there is no faster way to tell a reader that what you are doing does not
  matter.
- *"I" is fine.* Don't use the royal "we" on a sole-authored paper. "We" for you
  and the reader together is fine.
- *Present tense, and consistent tense.* Describe what you find in Table 5, not
  what you will find.
- *Define your symbols somewhere findable and give them names.* This is the one
  place a little repetition earns its keep.
- *Don't abbreviate authors' names.* You would want yours spelled out.
- *Don't open with a cute quotation, and don't overuse italics* - if emphasis
  needs marking, the sentence probably needs rewriting.
- *Much bad writing is an attempt to avoid responsibility for what you are
  saying.* That is where passives, "it should be noted that", and
  literature-first organization come from. Take a breath and take responsibility.

## 4. QJPS: the house style of a journal that cares

- *Three significant digits in tables and text.*
- *Standard errors rather than stars.* Readers can divide, and they have
  "inalienable rights to choose their own critical values".
- *Full descriptive variable names.* `DINVTSHR` is opaque; "Democratic
  incumbent's vote share" is clear.
- *No heading on the first section* - readers know an article begins with an
  introduction - and no roadmap paragraph.
- *Do not number sections* unless cross-referencing is frequent and essential.
- *Do not cite the uncontroversial.* "Members of Congress are reelection-seekers
  (Mayhew 1974)" and "the solution concept is Nash equilibrium (see Nash 1957)"
  are both citations nobody needed.
- *Present tense.* "Riker (1962) teaches us", not "taught"; and hunt down *will*.
- *Quotation marks only for genuine quotation.* Not for apology ("this
  'signaling' model"), not for scare-marking a technical term. Use italics to
  introduce or define a term.
- *because* is causal, *since* is temporal; *although* is concessive, *while* is
  temporal. Mixing them creates arguments you did not make.
- *Don't begin sentences with "There".* "There exists a controversial literature
  on x" is "The literature on x is controversial".
- *hopefully* means full of hope, not "I hope".
- *deploy* is not a synonym for *use*, unless the article really is about Humvees.
- *Table titles carry table-specific information only* - not descriptions that
  belong in the text, and not footnotes repeating what the article already says.

## 5. Sniderman: what the prose does

Sniderman published little explicit advice, so the lessons come from the work.
He is on the same shelf as Luskin and Kinder in this repository's list of
writers worth reading.

**The plain opening that is the entire agenda.** *The Scar of Race* (Sniderman
and Piazza, 1993) begins: "It is time to look again at how Americans think about
the issue of race." Thirteen words, none over two syllables. Subject, claim, and
stakes, with no literature review and no throat-clearing. The book starts where
a weaker book would arrive on page ten.

**Say the difficult thing flatly.** A reviewer's summary of the book's thesis
catches the register the authors use on themselves: "Lots of ordinary folks know
it, but it's not the sort of thing social scientists tend to say: when it comes
to questions of public policy, few whites are racists." Colloquial diction
aimed at a technical audience, and a claim stated without insulation. (Quoted
from a review rather than the book itself - attribute it that way.)

**Argument by counterpoint.** Two opposed accounts organize each chapter, so the
reader always knows which two things are being weighed and what would decide
between them. The structure does the work that signposting does badly.

**Design so the finding is legible without statistics.** Sniderman's survey
experiments - the list experiment, the counter-argument technique, the
"mousetrap" designs described in his chapter on the logic of the survey
experiment - are built so the result shows up as a difference in proportions
that a reader can see. The writing is clear partly because the design was
chosen to be explicable.

**And the failure mode, because a style guide that only praises teaches badly.**
Reviewers of *The Scar of Race* criticised precisely this manner as "an
expository style that stresses stark contrasts and bold conclusions rather than
the nuances and conditional factors" that the subject demands. The lesson stands
with the caution attached: the clarity comes from committing to a claim, and
committing to a claim can flatten a genuinely conditional finding. Use the
plainness; keep the conditionality that is real.

## 6. Luskin's own prose

Two things worth stealing.

**The title can carry the argument.** His 1991 *AJPS* reply on standardized
coefficients is titled *Abusus Non Tollit Usum* - abuse does not negate proper
use. Three words, and they are the thesis.

**Advice that obeys itself.** *Robert's Rules* is written under its own
constraints: short declaratives, one thought per sentence, ordinary words, a
metaphor only when it earns its place ("spend words as though they were money,
and you were a miser"). That the document is readable is the strongest available
evidence that the rules work. Any distillation of it, including this file,
should be checked the same way.
